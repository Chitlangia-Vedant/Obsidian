### **Recap: What you need to know**

- **Downlevel offers are often “close calls,” not determined by any objective measure,** but rather by a sum of subjective signals from recruiter screens, early interviews, and final-round interviews.
- **Most downlevels aren’t about coding rounds.** It’s system design and behavioral interviews that set your level.
- **It’s expensive:** Google pays senior engineers approximately $180K/year less than staff engineers. Accepting a downlevel offer can delay your growth by years.
### **What not to do**

- Don’t treat it like a comp negotiation. **Level comes first**. If you start talking salary or start date, you’ve implicitly accepted the level.
- Don’t respond passively. Burying your dissatisfaction at the end of a convoluted response is _not_ the same as leading with _“This is not the level I’m targeting.”_
### **What works**

- **Order of operations matters**. Discuss level → compensation → start date → paperwork.
- **Your immediate response matters**: Signal interest in the team, _not_ the offer. You can say: _“I’m excited about the team, but I don’t believe L5 reflects the impact I’ve delivered. Can we revisit the level decision?”_
- **Offer alternatives**: Ask to retake the round you underperformed in, speak with the hiring manager, escalate to a VP, or suggest another creative way to demonstrate signal for your target level.
- **Be persistent**. Call #1 will not end with a resolution. Your best-case scenario on the first call is hearing: _“Let me see what I can do.”_
### **Recap: The truth about downleveling**
Tech companies downlevel candidates when they think they can get away with it—and they often do.

Downleveling is rampant—in some job markets, Meta downlevels most engineering offers. Not because you failed the coding rounds; you didn’t show enough impact in the rounds that determine level.

Recruiters might say “you’ll get promoted fast.” _Often, that’s a lie_. Many engineers stay stuck and eventually leave without ever being promoted.

Levels aren’t standardized. A _staff engineer_ at Google may not equate to a _staff engineer_ at a startup. That makes the game fuzzy—and easy for manipulation.

**How to respond if you're downleveled**

- **Don’t talk about comp, start dates, or paperwork** until the level is clear.
- **Don’t be vague.** Say: _“This isn’t the level I’m targeting. Let’s align on that first.”_
- **Offer solutions**: Ask to retake a round, speak with the hiring manager, or suggest another step.
- **Don’t signal agreement** unless you mean it. Even polite excitement can be read as acceptance.
- **Be firm, but collaborative.** Balance pushback with genuine interest in the team.

The recruiter’s job is to close the deal. Your job is to slow it down, clarify your value, and shift the conversation from “sign now” to “let’s make sure this is the right fit.”
### **When to agree to a downlevel**
Only if:

- You're okay with trading off more compensation (and responsibility) in favor of getting a deal done sooner.
- The comp + team + role are above your expectations.
- You have no better BATNA (i.e., competing offers).
- You’re desperate to leave a bad situation _now._
### **Final thoughts**
If you want to reverse a downlevel, don’t be too nice. You’re negotiating. Bring receipts, stay calm, and aim for clarity.

And remember: “_Scared money don’t make no money_.”
